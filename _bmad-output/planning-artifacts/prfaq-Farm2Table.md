# Pastured eggs, sourdough, and honey from three Sarasota farms — one cart, one checkout, every producer named.

## For Sarasota families and seniors who want to know exactly who grew, raised, or made every item in their cart — without the warehouse middleman or another CSA mystery box.

**SARASOTA, FL — February 13, 2027** — Sarasota and Bradenton shoppers who want to buy farm-direct have been forced to choose between driving to multiple Saturday farmers markets, settling for warehouse "local" from grocery delivery, or signing up for a CSA mystery box they can't customize. Farm2Table opens today, letting them build a single online cart from dozens of named local farms — pastured eggs from one, sourdough from another, raw honey from a third — and pick it up, get it delivered, or have it shipped, depending on each farmer's preference.

Across the Gulf Coast, the people who care most about where their food comes from are also the ones with the least time or ability to chase it. A young family with two kids in weekend sports can't be at three farmers markets across Tampa, St. Pete, and Sarasota. A retired couple in their seventies can't carry a Saturday morning haul out of a packed parking lot the way they used to. Both have watched grocery delivery quietly turn "local" into a marketing word, and both have unsubscribed from CSA boxes after the third week of beets they didn't ask for.

Now, the same week looks different. The young parent fills one cart on Sunday night and eats from three named farms by Saturday. The retired couple finds the farmers she grew up with — and orders from them without leaving the kitchen table. Each farm sends its items the way it does business: pickup at the farm, a porch drop, a local delivery route, or a shipped box. The shopping happens once, the variety the CSA never offered shows up, and every item still names the farm behind it.

> "I built Farm2Table because I wanted to feed my family from farms I could name — and I couldn't find them all in one place. Most of the best ones never make it to a grocery store, a CSA box, or a single farmers market. There are dozens of farmers around Sarasota with exactly the right food, and dozens of households trying to find them. Farm2Table is the introduction. The relationships go where they go."
> — Jen Chartier, Founder, Farm2Table

### How It Works

1. **Browse farms by name, location, or what they grow.** Pull up Farm2Table on a phone or laptop and search the directory of Sarasota-area farms and artisan producers. Filter by what matters — pastured eggs, raw honey, regenerative practices, microgreens, sourdough — or jump straight to a specific farm.

2. **Build one cart from as many farms as you want.** Add a half-dozen items from one farm, two from another, one from a third. Every line shows the named farm that produced it.

3. **At checkout, choose fulfillment per farm.** Each vendor displays their available options — farm-direct pickup, local delivery in select zip codes, or shipping for shelf-stable goods. Pay once.

4. **Receive the order the way each farm offered it.** Some farms you pick up from at their farm stand; others deliver locally; some ship shelf-stable goods. Depending on what's in your cart, that may mean more than one pickup — but every farm gets paid, and every item still names who grew, raised, or made it.

> "I'd been driving my kids to the Saturday market in St. Pete for years to get pasture-raised eggs from one farm and stopping in Sarasota for sourdough from another. Now I do all that shopping at the kitchen table on Sunday — and I still know whose eggs they are."
> — Sarah K., Sarasota mom of three

## Customer FAQ

### Q1: If I still have to drive to multiple farms to pick up my order, why is this better than just going to the Saturday farmers market in Sarasota?

A: Farm2Table doesn't replace the Saturday farmers market — it solves problems the Saturday market doesn't.

Most farmers markets have 30-40 vendors. The Tampa Bay-Sarasota region has hundreds of small farms and food artisans. Most don't show up at your market — wrong day, wrong location, no booth slot, or just not a market vendor. Farm2Table is how you find and order from those farms.

Three things the market can't do that we can:

1. **Discovery beyond your closest market.** A regenerative beef farm in Plant City and a goat dairy near Myakka don't share booths at the Sarasota market. Most weekly market shoppers never find them. We do.

2. **Shopping on your time.** Saturday morning is the market's window, not yours. Order Sunday night, pick up — or have it delivered or shipped — when each farm offers it.

3. **Inventory you can plan around.** At the market, the eggs are sold out by 9am. With Farm2Table, you reserve before you go.

The trade-off is real: a multi-farm order may mean more than one pickup stop, depending on which farms you chose and what they offer for delivery or shipping. For some customers, that's worse than the market. For customers who want variety their local market doesn't carry — or who can't reliably make Saturday morning at all — it's a different equation. We exist for that group.

### Q2: How is this different from CSA boxes I've already tried, or from Misfits Market and Imperfect Foods? They all sell themselves as "farm to door" too.

A: Three different things, three different problems we don't have:

**CSA boxes** are a mystery — you get what the farm decides to put in it that week, often single-farm, often subscription, often "another boatload of chard" by week three. Farm2Table is the opposite of a mystery box: you choose what you want, from the farms you want, no subscription required, no commitment. Skip a week, change your order weekly, mix five farms in one cart.

**Misfits Market and Imperfect Foods** are warehouse-aggregated grocery delivery in a "local" wrapper. Their food ships from regional distribution centers; the "local" label is loose. Customers consistently report it feels like organic grocery delivery, not farm-direct. Farm2Table never touches the food — every item ships, drops, or gets picked up from the named farm that produced it.

**Farm2Table is the only one that's neither a subscription nor a warehouse.** It's a marketplace where local farmers list their inventory, you pick what you want from however many farms you like, and each farm fulfills directly. No middleman aggregating, no algorithm choosing, no auto-renewal.

### Q3: Why should I pay a platform fee on top of farm prices when I could just buy directly from each farm's website or Instagram?

A: You can absolutely buy direct from each farm's website or DM them on Instagram — and for one or two favorite farms, that's probably what makes sense. Here's what a platform fee buys you for everything else:

1. **One cart instead of five checkouts.** Most weekly orders span three to seven farms. Direct means juggling that many separate transactions, payment methods, and accounts every week.

2. **Discovery you can't get elsewhere.** Most small farms have minimal online presence and don't win Google searches. Farm2Table surfaces them in one searchable place.

3. **One place to fix it if something goes wrong.** Order didn't show up? Wrong item? You have one place to go for resolution, not eight DMs to chase.

The fee is a few percent per order. If you only ever buy from one farm, direct is fine. If you want variety from many, the platform pays for itself in time and friction saved.

### Q4: How do I actually know the food is from the farms you say it's from? What stops a vendor from listing "Farm Joe's pastured eggs" when they're really wholesale eggs from Sysco?

A: Honest answer: no platform can guarantee perfect honesty from every vendor — and any platform that says it can is lying. Here's what we actually do:

1. **Documented verification at onboarding, by vendor type.** Every vendor application requires a verified business address and the appropriate registration for their category:
   - **Farms:** state agricultural license or registration, plus USDA Local Food Directory listing where applicable
   - **Cottage food producers** (home-kitchen bakers, jam-makers, granola, dried herbs): state cottage food registration where required, plus business license or DBA
   - **Commercial food producers** (commercial-kitchen bakers, fermented goods, packaged shelf-stable): state commercial food permit (FDACS in Florida) plus business license
   - **Beekeepers, micro-producers, and others:** business license or DBA, plus any applicable state apiary or production registration

   We cross-check what we can against public records before approving.

2. **Third-party certifications shown on the listing.** Where vendors have USDA Organic, Animal Welfare Approved, Certified Naturally Grown, food handler certifications, cottage food compliance, or similar credentials, we verify them with the issuing body and display them on the vendor profile. You can filter by them.

3. **Vendor agreement with teeth.** Listings must come from the producer doing the listing. Misrepresentation — listing wholesale product as your own, mislabeling practices, false provenance — is grounds for immediate removal.

4. **Public accountability and customer reporting.** Every vendor profile shows their address, practices, and any certifications. Customers can report suspected misrepresentation directly — we investigate, and vendors whose listings get repeat complaints are removed.

We can't guarantee no vendor will ever cheat — no farmers market or marketplace can. But our verification process makes fraud hard at the front door, our agreement makes the consequences real, and customers have a direct way to flag what doesn't add up.

If you're choosing between us and a grocery store with a "local" sticker: at the grocery store, "local" is unenforced and unverified. With us, every vendor has a name, a registered address, listed practices, and a removal policy attached to every listing.

### Q5: What happens if I get sick from food I bought through Farm2Table — who's responsible? The farm? You?

A: Short answer: the farm or producer that made the food is the responsible party. Long answer:

1. **The vendor is the primary responsible party.** When you buy from a Farm2Table vendor, you're buying directly from that vendor — they grew, raised, or made the food, and they're responsible for it being safe to eat. Our vendor agreement requires every vendor to carry product liability insurance and to comply with all applicable food safety regulations (FDACS in Florida, USDA where applicable, cottage food rules, etc.).

2. **Farm2Table is a marketplace, not a food handler.** We don't take possession of the food. The transaction is between you and the vendor; we facilitate the introduction and the payment. That's the legal structure, and it's the same structure most marketplaces operate under.

3. **What we actually do if you get sick:**
   - Report it to us right away. We help you connect with the vendor, document the incident, and report to the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services (FDACS) for any potential foodborne illness investigation.
   - We pause or remove the vendor's listing while the issue is investigated.
   - If the vendor's product is found at fault, our vendor agreement provides for refund and pursuit of insurance claims.
   - Repeat or serious incidents result in permanent removal from the platform.

4. **What our vendor agreement requires.** Every vendor must carry minimum product liability insurance, attest to compliance with food safety law, allow us to investigate complaints, and indemnify Farm2Table against claims arising from their products.

We're not a grocery store, and we're not a CSA — we're a marketplace, and the responsibility chain reflects that. But we don't disappear when something goes wrong; we have a process and a vendor agreement built to handle it.

### Q6: What happens to my account, payment info, and any standing orders if Farm2Table shuts down? You're a brand-new platform.

A: Fair concern — and worth being upfront about. Farm2Table is a new platform; we'd rather you know how this is structured than guess.

1. **Your payment information is held by Stripe, not Farm2Table.** We use Stripe Connect, the same payment infrastructure used by Shopify, Lyft, DoorDash, and thousands of other marketplaces. If Farm2Table shut down tomorrow, your payment information would still be safely managed by Stripe — we don't store credit card numbers ourselves.

2. **There are no subscriptions to get stranded.** Farm2Table doesn't auto-renew or charge you on a recurring schedule. You pay only for the orders you place, when you place them. There's nothing to cancel.

3. **Your data is yours.** You can export your order history and account information at any time from your account settings. If we ever wound down, we'd give a minimum 30-day notice and provide bulk export tools.

4. **Your relationships with the farms continue without us.** Every vendor profile includes the farm's name, address, and (where they share it) their direct contact info or website. If Farm2Table went away, the farm you discovered through us doesn't. You can keep buying from them directly.

We'd rather make this answerable up front than pretend the question doesn't exist for a new platform. Most of what makes Farm2Table valuable — the farms, the relationships, your own purchase history — survives us not surviving.

### Q7: What's the actual total cost — minimum order, service fees, delivery charges? And are prices higher than the farmers market?

A: Vendors set their own prices on Farm2Table, so what you pay reflects what each individual farm charges. Here's the breakdown of what shows up at checkout:

1. **Item prices** — set by each vendor. Most vendors price comparably to what they charge at the farmers market or on their own website. A few may price slightly higher; you'll see the price clearly before adding anything to your cart.

2. **Service fee** — a small percentage added at checkout, clearly shown before you confirm.

3. **Pickup, delivery, or shipping costs** — set by each vendor. Pickup at the farm is usually free. Local delivery and shipping have fees the vendor sets and displays at checkout.

4. **No minimum order from us.** Individual vendors may set their own minimums (especially for shipping), and that's shown on their listing.

Compared to the farmers market, total cost per item will usually be in the same range — sometimes the same, sometimes slightly higher. What the small premium pays for is discovery (farms you wouldn't otherwise find), shopping convenience (one cart, one checkout), and ordering on your time.

### Q8: How fresh is the food? When did it leave the farm? Am I picking up something that was harvested three days ago?

A: Freshness depends on the vendor and the product, but here's what you can generally expect:

1. **Farm-direct pickup**: usually the freshest option. Eggs gathered that morning, produce harvested the day before or that day, baked goods made within 24-48 hours. The farm knows when you're picking up and times their fulfillment to match.

2. **Local delivery**: typically harvested or made within 24-48 hours of delivery. The farm batches deliveries so things don't sit.

3. **Shipping** depends on what each vendor is licensed and equipped to ship, and which state they're in:
   - **Shelf-stable commercial goods** (honey, jam, dried herbs, granola, packaged hot sauces from FDACS-permitted commercial vendors): nationwide where the vendor offers it.
   - **Cottage food goods** (home-kitchen baked goods, jams, granola): rules vary by the vendor's state. Some states (PA, ND, TX, GA, TN) explicitly allow interstate shipping under their cottage food laws. Florida is conservative — most FL cottage food vendors will offer in-state shipping only. Each vendor's listing shows where they ship to.
   - **Perishables** (meat, dairy, eggs, fresh produce): vendors with appropriate USDA/FDACS licensing can ship using insulated coolers, ice packs or dry ice, and overnight or 2-day shipping. Many small farms don't have the licenses required for interstate shipping of these products; some do.
   - Each vendor's listing shows what shipping methods they offer, where they ship to, and any special handling requirements.

Compared to a grocery store, the food on Farm2Table doesn't sit on a shelf for days waiting for you to buy it — vendors fulfill against your specific order. Compared to a farmers market, the freshness is usually similar (the same farms supplying both), and sometimes fresher because you're picking up directly from the source instead of from a stall that may have been set up since 5am.

Each vendor's listing shows their typical lead time and fulfillment schedule. If a specific item has a "made fresh" or "harvested for your order" promise from the vendor, you'll see it on the product page.

### Q9: What if I order from three farms and one cancels at the last minute — do I still pay for the others? Do I make a separate trip for the partial order?

A: Each farm fulfills its part of your order independently — that's the underlying structure of a multi-farm cart. So if one vendor cancels:

1. **You're refunded immediately for the cancelled items.** Refund processes through Stripe back to your original payment method, typically within 1-3 business days.

2. **The rest of your order proceeds as planned.** The other farms still fulfill their items on their original schedule — pickup, delivery, or shipping as you originally chose. They've already started preparing your order; one farm cancelling doesn't affect their work.

3. **You get notified, and we show you other farms offering the same thing.** Lost the bacon? We'll surface which other Farm2Table vendors carry pastured bacon so you can add it to a new order if you want — different farm, similar product. The cancellation becomes a way to discover another producer rather than a dead end.

4. **No extra trip forced on you.** Because each vendor was already going to fulfill independently — at their farm, on their delivery route, or via shipping — a cancellation just means one less pickup or one less package, not a new logistics problem.

Vendors who cancel often or last-minute have it tracked in their account. Repeat patterns trigger review and can result in temporary suspension or removal from the platform — same accountability mechanism as misrepresentation.

### Q10: What if I'm a snowbird, here for six months and gone for six? Or out of town the week of my pickup?

A: Two different scenarios, two different answers:

1. **Snowbirds (in Sarasota for part of the year, away the rest):** No subscription means there's nothing to pause. When you're in town, log in and order. When you're away, the account just sits — your purchase history, saved farms, and preferences are still there when you come back. If you want to keep buying shelf-stable goods (honey, jam, dried herbs) from your favorite Sarasota farms while you're up north, you can have them shipped to your summer address — if the vendor ships to that area.

   And if you spend your other half of the year somewhere we don't yet operate — let us know. We're expanding hub by hub, and customer demand is one of the biggest signals we use to decide where to launch next.

2. **Out of town the week of your pickup:** A few options:
   - **Cancel before the cutoff.** Each vendor sets their fulfillment cutoff (usually 24-48 hours before pickup or delivery). Cancel before then and you're refunded; the vendor hasn't started preparing your order yet.
   - **Ask the vendor to hold the order.** If you reach out before the cutoff, many farms will hold your order for the following week — you don't lose the purchase, the vendor still gets paid, and your food is waiting when you're back.
   - **After the cutoff:** the vendor has already started preparing your order, and cancellation is at their discretion. Some will refund or hold; some can't. The cleanest move is adjusting your pickup timing before placing the order rather than after.

No commitments. No subscriptions. The platform doesn't care whether you order weekly, monthly, or twice a year.

### Q4: What's the hardest thing to build — multi-farm cart with per-vendor fulfillment, the substitute matching engine, marketplace tax compliance, vendor agreement enforcement, all of it. Do you have a plan to actually build this — yourself, contractor, no-code stack? What's the technical risk?

### Q6: What's your regulatory exposure? FDACS oversight, marketplace facilitator sales tax (every FL sale once you hit $100K), vendor liability transfer, cottage food law specifics, FSMA 204 traceability. What could the state shut down if you got it wrong?

A: Regulatory exposure breaks down into six real risk areas, with mitigations:

**1. Florida marketplace facilitator sales tax** (highest immediate risk once GMV scales)
- FL law: marketplace facilitators must collect and remit sales tax on all sales once **$100K** in taxable remote sales threshold is hit (FL uses revenue-only threshold; no transaction-count alternative exists in FL law). Likely triggered in Year 2 at $1M GMV.
- Most farm-direct products are grocery-exempt (eggs, produce, raw honey, meat), but prepared foods (sourdough, pickled goods, hot sauces) are taxable.
- **Mitigation:** Stripe Tax integration with proper product tax code mapping. Tax compliance gets handled per-transaction at checkout. Farm2Table still needs to register as a marketplace facilitator with FL Dept of Revenue once threshold is crossed.

**2. Vendor product liability** (moderate likelihood, moderate severity)
- Food poisoning, allergic reactions, contamination — Section 230 does NOT reliably shield platforms from product liability claims for physical injury when the platform plays a role in the sale, distribution, fulfillment, labeling, or transaction chain. (The widely-cited Oberdorf v. Amazon panel decision was vacated on en banc rehearing, but courts in multiple jurisdictions continue to treat marketplaces as potential "sellers" under state-specific strict product liability theories.)
- **Mitigation:** vendor agreement with reps/warranties + indemnification + hold-harmless + insurance requirement. Customer ToS includes arbitration + class-action waiver. Founder LLC structure protects personal assets. Marketplace-facilitator framing positions Farm2Table as merchant service provider, not distributor.

**3. FDACS / state ag compliance** (vendor primarily liable)
- Cottage food law violations (mislabeling, TCS items, interstate shipping ambiguity): vendor's responsibility per agreement.
- Commercial food permit requirements: vendor's responsibility per onboarding documentation.
- **Egg producers:** ALL FL egg vendors require an FDACS Limited Poultry and Egg Farm Operation permit even at small scale. No general "small backyard exemption" exists in FL law — the framework uses dozen-per-week thresholds (around 30 dozen/week trigger) and licensing categories. Egg vendor onboarding must verify permit.
- **Meat producers:** USDA-exempt or USDA-inspected, in-state direct-to-consumer only for exempt; interstate requires USDA inspection.
- **Beekeepers:** FDACS apiary registration required regardless of scale + cottage food OR commercial permit for sales side.
- **Raw dairy:** pet-only sale in FL; effectively excluded from platform.
- **Mitigation:** tiered vendor onboarding requires appropriate state registrations (per Customer FAQ Q4). Vendor agreement attests to compliance. Customer reporting + investigation handles violations.

**4. Interstate shipping (state-by-state ambiguity)**
- **FL cottage food interstate:** ambiguous under current law. FL Stat. § 500.80 conservative reading suggests in-state only; some UF/IFAS guidance suggests interstate may be allowed. Defaults to in-state only until FDACS confirms broader scope.
- **PA Limited Food Establishment (LFE):** allows interstate shipping for non-TCS foods (longstanding program, not a recent 2025 expansion).
- **ND, GA, TN, TX:** recent (2025) state expansions explicitly allow interstate cottage food shipping under their respective frameworks.
- **Meat/dairy interstate:** requires USDA inspection (most small farms exempt for in-state direct-to-consumer only).
- **Shelf-stable goods from FDACS-commercial-permitted vendors:** generally OK for interstate.
- **Mitigation:** vendor onboarding asks about shipping capabilities; platform respects vendor-set shipping coverage; vendor agreement requires compliance with applicable laws.

**5. FSMA 204 traceability + FDA registration** (low immediate; competitive moat opportunity by July 2028)
- Federal Food Safety Modernization Act traceability rule applies to certain foods; deadline July 2028.
- Most small farms exempt under <$25K threshold.
- FDA facility registration (21 CFR Part 1, Subpart H) may be required for vendors shipping food interstate.
- **Mitigation:** Build basic traceability into vendor onboarding from day one; deeper compliance becomes a Y2-3 product investment.

**6. Vendor non-compliance liability** (residual platform exposure)
- Marketplace can have residual exposure if vendors lie about licensing, ship without proper permits, or violate state laws.
- **Primary mitigations:** strong vendor agreement (reps, warranties, indemnification, insurance requirement), documented verification at onboarding, marketplace-facilitator framing in ToS, active enforcement on customer reports, liability insurance.
- **Real risk profile:**
  - Vendor lies and ships unlicensed → vendor primary liability; Farm2Table risk low if verification was done in good faith.
  - Customer harmed by vendor product → vendor primary; Farm2Table named in suit; insurance + agreement structure provides defense.
  - State AG action over pattern of violations → real exposure if Farm2Table didn't act on knowledge; manageable if visibly compliant.
- **Operational requirement:** Cannot be willfully blind. If reports come in, investigate and act. Documented enforcement protects against negligence claims.
- **Commits to:** real vendor agreement (~$2-5K of legal budget), active enforcement process, customer reporting channel, liability insurance.

**What could the state actually shut down?**
- FDACS could order Farm2Table to remove vendors who repeatedly violate food safety regs (manageable — same enforcement mechanism as Customer FAQ Q4).
- Florida Dept of Revenue could pursue back sales tax + penalties if marketplace facilitator obligations aren't met (avoided with Stripe Tax + FL DOR registration).
- Federal FDA could pursue Farm2Table for facilitating interstate shipping of restricted products (avoided with vendor-set shipping coverage + vendor agreement compliance).
- State AG could pursue Farm2Table for facilitating unlicensed sales at scale (avoided with verified onboarding + active enforcement).

No single regulatory issue is a launch blocker. The vendor agreement + Stripe Tax + tiered onboarding + active enforcement cover the majority of exposure. The remainder is reactive (vendor violations, customer complaints) handled through the same agreement framework.

**Real budget for legal/compliance Y1:** $3-10K — covers LLC, vendor agreement (must be teeth-strong, not template), ToS, FDACS compliance review, insurance setup. **Strong recommendation: get a single consultation with a food/marketplace attorney before launch (~$300-500) to review the vendor agreement and onboarding process.** Marginal cost, significant risk reduction.
